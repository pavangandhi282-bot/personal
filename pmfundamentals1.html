Who does the business serve?

Classify the transaction.

B2B means business to business. B2C means business to consumer. The same company can serve both markets through different offerings.

Market classifier

Common tendencies, not fixed rules
DimensionB2BB2C
Who buysAn organizationAn individual consumer
Decision processOften several stakeholders and a longer sales cycleOften fewer stakeholders and a shorter purchase decision
Buyer and userOften different peopleOften the same person, with exceptions
Product concernsWorkflow fit, reliability, integrations, security, and return on investmentConvenience, price, habit, enjoyment, and personal value

Avoid memorizing “B2B is easy” or “B2C needs natural product sense” as rules. Job availability and entry difficulty depend on the company, market, role, and your experience.

Understand before solving

Make assumptions visible.

You cannot eliminate every assumption. Name the risky ones, collect evidence, and avoid treating a proposed solution as a proven need.

Problem space or solution space?

Turn a claim into a test

Claim
Daily reminders will bring learners back.

Hidden assumption
Forgetting is the main reason learners stop.

First investigation
Speak with learners who stopped and examine where they dropped off.

Possible experiment
If evidence supports forgetting as a cause, test optional reminders against a comparison group.

Success and guardrails
Look for more useful lesson returns while checking notification opt-outs and complaints.

Primary research

Gather new evidence directly

Interview users, observe their workflow, or run usability sessions. Ask about actual past behavior rather than only what they say they might do.

Secondary research

Learn from existing evidence

Review existing studies, published reports, previous research, and competitor information. Check whether the evidence applies to your users and context.

Research reduces uncertainty. It does not mean you must know everything before experimenting. Start with the assumption that would most seriously undermine the idea if it were false.
Separate purchasing from usage

Who buys it? Who uses it?

For this revision exercise, the customer is the buyer and the user is the person using the product. One person can fill both roles.

Role detective

Why the difference matters

A parent buying a toy may care about price, durability, and safety. The child may care about fun.

A company purchasing software may care about cost and security. Employees using it may care about speed and ease of use.

Research both perspectives

A product can be easy to buy but frustrating to use, or loved by users but difficult for a buyer to approve.

In real organizations, buyer, payer, decision-maker, administrator, and user may be separate roles. Start by identifying them rather than assuming.
